Principle

A Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) measures linear displacement. It has one primary winding in the middle and two identical secondary windings on either side. A movable ferromagnetic core slides inside. The primary is driven by AC, and the core changes how strongly the primary couples to each secondary.

Working

Primary: Vp = Vm sin(ωt), typically 1–10 V at 1–10 kHz
Secondary EMFs: e₁ = M₁ · dIp/dt e₂ = M₂ · dIp/dt
Secondaries in series opposition: Vo = e₁ − e₂ = (M₁ − M₂) · dIp/dt
  • Core at centre (x = 0): M₁ = M₂, so e₁ = e₂ and Vo ≈ 0. This is the null position.
  • Core toward S₁ (x > 0): M₁ > M₂, so Vo is larger and in phase with the reference.
  • Core toward S₂ (x < 0): M₂ > M₁, so Vo has the same size for the same distance but is 180° out of phase.

The magnitude of Vo tells you how far the core has moved, and its phase tells you which way.

Sensitivity and linearity

Vo ≈ S · Vp · x (within the linear range)

The sensitivity S is usually quoted in mV/V/mm, meaning output per volt of excitation per millimetre. Doubling Vp doubles the output in mV/mm, but S in mV/V/mm stays the same. Near the ends of the stroke the core leaves the windings and the curve flattens, so the response is no longer a straight line. In this lab the linear range (within 0.5 % of reading) is about ±8.3 mm.

Signal conditioning

Oscillator → LVDT → amplifier → phase-sensitive detector → low-pass filter → DC voltage

A plain AC voltmeter only reads the size of Vo, so it cannot tell left from right. A phase-sensitive detector (demodulator) multiplies Vo by a reference in phase with the carrier. After low-pass filtering, the DC output is proportional to x and its sign gives the direction.

Vdc = Vo cos(Δφ) (Δφ = phase error of the reference)

Phase and frequency

Because the primary is inductive, the output leads the excitation by a phase angle that falls as frequency rises. The output also grows with frequency up to a plateau. That is why the auto-align option exists, and why the manual phase setting must be matched to the output phase.

Residual null voltage

At the centre the output is never exactly zero. Slight winding imbalance and harmonics leave a small residual voltage, usually 90° out of phase with the main signal. A phase-sensitive detector rejects most of it.

Advantages and limits

  • No sliding electrical contact, so there is no wear and the life is very long.
  • Practically infinite resolution, limited by the electronics.
  • Rugged, and tolerant of dirt, moisture and temperature.
  • Needs AC excitation and demodulation electronics.
  • Response is linear only over part of the stroke, and the core must be aligned.

Applications

Position feedback in machine tools, thickness gauges, pressure and force transducers (with a bellows or diaphragm), level measurement, and valve position sensing.

Explore the LVDT

  1. Leave excitation at 3 V and 2.5 kHz. Set the core to 0 mm with the Centre button. Note S₁, S₂ and Vo.
  2. Move the core to +5 mm, then −5 mm. Compare the sizes of S₁ and S₂ and look at the phase of Vo.
  3. Open the Oscilloscope tab. Watch the Vo trace flip by 180° as the core crosses the centre.
  4. Increase Vp. Observe that the output in mV/mm rises, but the output per volt does not.
  5. Change the frequency. See how the amplitude and the phase of Vo change.
  6. Drive the core beyond ±8 mm and see the output curve bend away from the straight line.

Calibrate and find linearity

  1. Set Vp and f and do not change them during calibration.
  2. Move the core to −14 mm and press Record reading. Repeat in 2 mm steps up to +14 mm. (Auto-fill sweep does this for you as a shortcut.)
  3. Open Characteristics. See the recorded points on the curve and the shaded linear range.
  4. In Observations choose the range of |x| for the fit. Read the sensitivity in mV/mm, the maximum deviation and the linearity error as a percentage of full scale.
  5. Repeat the fit with 6 mm and 12 mm limits. Explain why the linearity error changes.

Measure an unknown displacement

  1. Complete a calibration first, using the same Vp and f you will use here.
  2. Read the demodulated DC output from the meter.
  3. Calculate x = Vdc ÷ S, using your own sensitivity in mV/mm.
  4. Type your answer and press Check answer. Compare it with the true value.
  5. Press New displacement and try again, this time with a different excitation to see why you must recalibrate.

Precautions

  • Keep the core inside the linear range for accurate measurements.
  • Do not change excitation voltage or frequency between calibration and measurement.
  • Allow the electronics to warm up in a real lab, since sensitivity drifts with temperature.

Welcome to the LVDT Lab

An LVDT turns the position of a metal core into an AC voltage. The size of that voltage shows how far the core has moved, and its phase shows which way.

  1. Pick an experiment from the three buttons at the top.
  2. Follow the blue “Next step” box above the diagram. It updates as you work.
  3. Record readings in Observations, then test yourself in the Viva quiz.

Words you will see

  • Core: the movable iron rod. Drag it in the diagram or use the slider.
  • Primary / secondary: the middle coil is driven by AC. The two side coils produce the output.
  • Null: the centre position where the output is nearly zero.
  • Demodulated DC: the signed voltage after the phase-sensitive detector. Its sign gives direction.
